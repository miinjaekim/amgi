# 군사용어 — 부대·참모 / Military Terms: Unit & Staff — Draft for Review

**Proposed pack name.** `military-unit`, shown as **Military Terms: Unit &
Staff** in an English UI and **군사용어 — 부대·참모** in a Korean one. Both
sides have to be direction-neutral, because this pack runs in both directions:
"Military English" would be wrong for the English-native learner studying
Korean, and 군사용어 is what the field calls this material anyway.

**One of two.** This pack was split out of a single 474-term draft. Its sibling
is [군사용어 — 안보·정세](military-affairs-pack-draft.md), the register of a
briefing and a press statement. The two share an audience and a card shape but
almost no vocabulary, and the split is by *register*, not difficulty — neither
is the beginner one. Cross-references below to the sibling pack are written
「안보·정세 §n」.

**Concept:** a term-pair glossary for the job a **Korean military English
interpreter** actually does — sitting between a ROK unit and a US one and
having to produce the *other side's* word for something, immediately, out loud.
Not a reading list. The unit of study is the pair (대대 ↔ battalion), which is
why this is the first pack proposed to run **in both directions**: the same
authored list registered under English for a Korean-native learner and under
Korean for an English-native one.

**Who it's for.** 통역병 and KATUSA soldiers, ROK officers posted to CFC/UNC or
to a combined staff, and — reading the other way — US personnel assigned to
Korea who need the Korean term their counterpart will actually say. Both
populations exist in the app already: this pack is the first one where they are
the *same* content rather than two different curricula.

**The register.** A unit and a combined staff. Ranks, echelons, orders, radio
procedure, supply, casualty handling, barracks life — what an interpreter needs
standing next to a US counterpart, in a motor pool or on a net or at a change
of command. The failure mode here is stumbling. In the sibling pack it is
saying something wrong in a way the room can quote.

**Selection filter:** terms where knowing the concept does not give you the
word. A bilingual soldier can describe a fragmentary order in either language
and still be useless in the room if they cannot say 단편명령 / FRAGO on
demand. Terms where the translation is transparent and nobody stumbles (군인 /
soldier, 총 / gun, 훈련 / training) are deliberately out. What's in: fixed
doctrinal terms, acronyms that are used as words, alliance and armistice
proper nouns, radio procedure — and the traps, which are the reason a glossary
beats a dictionary here.

**Context hints** carry more weight in this pack than in any previous one.
About a third of the entries have one, and in §10 the hint *is* the entry — the
whole content of 병장 for an interpreter is that the official chart says
"Sergeant" and the authority the US listener hears in that word is not there.
Hints survive onto the saved card as `briefDefinition`, so a depth call made
months later still explains the military sense of 작전 rather than the everyday
one.

**Sources:** 합동참모본부 「합동·연합작전 군사용어사전」 for the doctrinal
Korean and its published English; the 국문·영문 editions of 국방부's
[국방백서](https://www.mnd.go.kr) read side by side, which is where the official
ROK–US rank equivalency and the English names of ROK organizations come from;
the US [DoD Dictionary of Military and Associated Terms](https://www.jcs.mil)
for the US half; and MND / USFK / CFC English-language releases for exercise
names and alliance bodies as they are currently written. All unclassified,
all published. **Assembled by hand from those, not scraped** — which is why
this file exists.

**Coverage check.** This list was reconciled against a widely circulated
어학병/통역장교 선발 preparation glossary (359 entries, V4.16, April 2025)
built for the selection exam's news-interpretation task. The overlap was
roughly fifty concepts — under a quarter of either list — and nearly everything
that glossary covered and this one did not became the sibling pack. What it
did surface here was a real error: §1 gave Army rank equivalents with no note
that they are service-specific, and a 통역장교 can be assigned to any service.
It also supplied the 군단 pronunciation hint and 작전, which the preamble
above had been referencing since the first draft without the list ever
carrying it.

**Accuracy caveat, read before the list.** Military terminology in Korea has
moved recently and in ways that strand older glossaries: 헌병 became 군사경찰 in
2020, 영창 was abolished the same year, and the combined exercise names were
reworked in 2023. I have written what I believe is current, but this list needs
a pass from someone who has served recently or works on a combined staff now.
Corrections on §10 especially are the most valuable thing a reviewer can give.

## 1. 계급·호칭 — Ranks and forms of address

The equivalency chart, plus the two ranks that need a warning attached. This is
§1 because it is the first thing an interpreter gets wrong in public.

**The chart below is Army, and so are the Air Force and Marine Corps. The Navy
is not.** ROK Navy officer ranks map to a different set of English words —
소위 Ensign, 중위 Lieutenant Junior Grade, 대위 Lieutenant, 소령 Lieutenant

## 10. 통역 함정 — Traps

The section that justifies the pack. These are terms where a correct-looking
translation misleads the listener, or where the Korean concept has no US
counterpart and the interpreter has to gloss rather than translate. Every entry
is hinted, because here the hint is the content.

**Some traps are not listed here, on purpose.** 병장, 부사관 and 상황실 belong
in this section by nature but live in §1 and §3, carrying their full trap hint
there. 대위/대령 in §1 and 군단 in §2 are two more. **Three of this section's
closest relatives are in the sibling pack** and cannot be repeated here without
authoring the same pair twice: 취역식/임관식 in 안보·정세 §6, where two
unrelated events share one English name; 전역(戰役) in §8, a homograph of the
전역 in §7 above; and 제병협동 in §8, where "combined arms" uses the English
word this section spends its first two rows separating from 합동, and means
neither. If the two packs ever merge back, those three come home to §10. A term cannot appear in two sections: both rows would author the same
pair, and two cards sharing a back make back-to-front review unanswerable —
the same constraint that forced the 초래하다/야기하다 splits on TOPIK. Where a
trap has a home elsewhere, the home wins and the hint travels with it.

| 한국어 | English | context hint |
|---|---|---|
| 연합 | combined | multinational, as in 한미연합사 — this is the one to keep straight from 합동 |
| 합동 | joint | multi-service within one nation, as in 합동참모본부 |
| 군사경찰 | military police | renamed from 헌병 in 2020 — 헌병 still gets said, and dates the speaker |

## Open questions

- **The bidirectional registration is the real decision here, and it is a code
  change this PR deliberately does not make.** `VOCAB_PACKS` keys on
  `StudyLanguage`, and `buildPackCardDraft` writes the study side last so it
  wins — which means one authored list of pairs can produce both packs for free:
  registered under `English` the Korean side is the back, registered under
  `Korean` the English side is. No new pack shape is needed, only a source of
  pairs and two derived `VocabPack`s. What needs deciding is whether that shows
  up as two decks with different names, or one deck the app orients from the
  user's study language.
- **Four registrations, four ids.** With the 부대/정세 split, this pack and its
  sibling each register twice, so there are four `VocabPack`s in play:
  `military-unit-ko` / `military-unit-en` and `military-affairs-ko` /
  `military-affairs-en`. They cannot share ids across directions.
  `getCollectionId` returns `card.packId` unqualified, so a shared id would
  collapse cards saved from the Korean-study deck and the English-study deck
  into one collection on `/cards` — which contradicts the position taken in the
  next question. The display `name` is shared across each direction pair.
- **Cross-direction saved marking.** `collectSavedTerms` collects the study
  field and `term`, so a user who saved 대대 from the Korean deck will *not* see
  `battalion` marked as saved on the English one. I think that is correct —
  front-to-back in the other direction is a genuinely different card, and this
  pack's whole premise is that both directions are worth drilling — but it is
  the first pack where the two decks overlap by construction, so it should be a
  decision rather than a side effect.
- **Acronyms: which side is the card?** OPCON, FRAGO, MEDEVAC and SOFA are used
  as words, not abbreviations. I have written the English side as
  `expansion (ACRONYM)` throughout, which makes the back long and the review
  answer ambiguous — "fragmentary order" and "FRAGO" are both right. The
  alternative is the acronym alone with the expansion in `context`, which reads
  better on a card and worse in a list. This pack can tolerate either; the
  sibling cannot, and §3 and §4 there settle it.
- **Is §7 and §10's barracks vocabulary in scope?** 자대, 말년, 점호 and 소원수리
  are not military English in the doctrinal sense; they are what an interpreter
  is asked about when a US NCO wants to understand a ROK soldier's situation.
  I think that is exactly the job, but it is the least defensible part of the
  list and the easiest to drop as a section.
- **Currency, and who reviews it.** The 2020 renames are in here, and there are
  probably others I have missed. This wants a reviewer who has served recently
  or works a combined staff now — more than any pack we have shipped, this one
  goes stale.
- **`pronounceable` is yes, and one hint is carrying work it should not have
  to.** 군단 corps exists half as a pronunciation warning. A Korean interpreter
  has to say "howitzer" out loud and an English-native learner has to say
  곡사포; the flag exists per pack rather than per direction, so we set it
  independently on each registration.
- **Ordering against the sibling.** These two are not a sequence — a 통역병 in a
  line unit wants this pack first, a 통역장교 headed for public affairs wants
  안보·정세 first — but the deck page has no way to say "either, depending". If
  it lists them in a fixed order, that order is a recommendation whether we mean
  it as one or not.
