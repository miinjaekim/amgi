# Korean–English Military Terms Pack — Draft for Review

**Concept:** a term-pair glossary for the job a **Korean military English
interpreter** actually does — sitting between a ROK unit and a US one and
having to produce the *other side's* word for something, immediately, out loud.
Not a reading list. The unit of study is the pair (대대 ↔ battalion), which is
why this is the first pack proposed to run **in both directions**: the same
authored list registered under English for a Korean-native learner and under
Korean for an English-native one.

**Who it's for.** 통역병 and KATUSA soldiers, ROK officers posted to CFC/UNC or
to a combined staff, and — reading the other way — US personnel assigned to
Korea who need the Korean term their counterpart will actually say. Both
populations exist in the app already: this pack is the first one where they are
the *same* content rather than two different curricula.

**Selection filter:** terms where knowing the concept does not give you the
word. A bilingual soldier can describe a fragmentary order in either language
and still be useless in the room if they cannot say 단편명령 / FRAGO on
demand. Terms where the translation is transparent and nobody stumbles (군인 /
soldier, 총 / gun, 훈련 / training) are deliberately out. What's in: fixed
doctrinal terms, acronyms that are used as words, alliance and armistice
proper nouns, radio procedure — and the traps, which are the reason a glossary
beats a dictionary here.

**Two registers, two parts.** The list splits along a line that turned out to
matter more than any section boundary:

- **Part A (§1–10) — 부대 언어.** The register of a unit and a combined staff.
  Ranks, echelons, orders, radio procedure, supply, casualty handling, barracks
  life. This is what an interpreter needs standing next to a US counterpart.
- **Part B (§11–20) — 정세 언어.** The register of a briefing, a press
  statement and a news article. Alliance politics, the North Korean threat
  picture, missiles and nuclear, ROK defense systems and their acquisition
  agencies, named ministries, naval and air forces, maritime sovereignty, the
  character of modern warfare, commemoration, and overseas deployments.

An interpreter who can run a radio net but cannot say 확장억제 or 이동식
발사대 will fail on the first morning of a press engagement, and the reverse
soldier will fail on the first afternoon in a motor pool. Both halves are the
job. Whether they are one pack or two is now the largest open question below.

**Context hints** carry more weight in this pack than in any previous one.
About a third of the entries have one, and in §10 the hint *is* the entry — the
whole content of 병장 for an interpreter is that the official chart says
"Sergeant" and the authority the US listener hears in that word is not there.
Hints survive onto the saved card as `briefDefinition`, so a depth call made
months later still explains the military sense of 작전 rather than the everyday
one.

**Sources:** 합동참모본부 「합동·연합작전 군사용어사전」 for the doctrinal
Korean and its published English; the 국문·영문 editions of 국방부's
[국방백서](https://www.mnd.go.kr) read side by side, which is where the official
ROK–US rank equivalency and the English names of ROK organizations come from;
the US [DoD Dictionary of Military and Associated Terms](https://www.jcs.mil)
for the US half; and MND / USFK / CFC English-language releases for exercise
names and alliance bodies as they are currently written. All unclassified,
all published. **Assembled by hand from those, not scraped** — which is why
this file exists.

**Coverage check.** Part B exists because Part A was reconciled against a
widely circulated 어학병/통역장교 선발 preparation glossary (359 entries,
V4.16, April 2025) built for the selection exam's news-interpretation task.
The overlap between that list and the original draft was roughly fifty
concepts — under a quarter of either. Everything it covered that Part A did
not is now §11–20, re-filtered through the selection rule above: its 중동 /
the Middle East and 난민 / refugee did not survive, its 이동식 발사대 and
무해통항권 did. Three of its glosses were not carried over as written — it
renders 한미 연례 연합 군사연습 as a "joint military exercise" (its own
separate entries have 연합 = combined and 합동 = joint, so this contradicts
itself, and §10 here is the reason why), it gives 지대지/지대함/지대공 as
"ground-to-", where US usage is "surface-to-", and it glosses 군무원 as "army
civilians". Where the two disagreed on currency, the more recent reading won.

**Accuracy caveat, read before the list.** Military terminology in Korea has
moved recently and in ways that strand older glossaries: 헌병 became 군사경찰 in
2020, 영창 was abolished the same year, and the combined exercise names were
reworked in 2023. I have written what I believe is current, but this list needs
a pass from someone who has served recently or works on a combined staff now.
Corrections on §10 especially are the most valuable thing a reviewer can give —
and in Part B, on §14, where the three-axis system's English names are set by
MND and change with policy.

## 10. 통역 함정 — Traps

The section that justifies the pack. These are terms where a correct-looking
translation misleads the listener, or where the Korean concept has no US
counterpart and the interpreter has to gloss rather than translate. Every entry
is hinted, because here the hint is the content.

**Some traps are not listed here, on purpose.** 병장, 부사관 and 상황실 belong
in this section by nature but live in §1 and §3, carrying their full trap hint
there. Part B added four more of the same kind: 대위/대령 in §1, 군단 in §2,
취역식/임관식 in §16, and 제병협동 in §18 — where "combined arms" uses the
English word this section spends its first two rows separating from 합동,
and means neither. A term cannot appear in two sections: both rows would author the same
pair, and two cards sharing a back make back-to-front review unanswerable —
the same constraint that forced the 초래하다/야기하다 splits on TOPIK. Where a
trap has a home elsewhere, the home wins and the hint travels with it.

| 한국어 | English | context hint |
|---|---|---|
| 연합 | combined | multinational, as in 한미연합사 — this is the one to keep straight from 합동 |
| 합동 | joint | multi-service within one nation, as in 합동참모본부 |
| 군사경찰 | military police | renamed from 헌병 in 2020 — 헌병 still gets said, and dates the speaker |

## Part B — 정세 언어

Part A is the register of a room with two soldiers in it. Part B is the
register of a room with a microphone in it: a commander's press engagement, a
combined staff briefing, a KBS defense segment being interpreted live. The
vocabulary is almost disjoint from Part A's, and the failure mode is different
— in Part A you stumble, in Part B you say something that is wrong in a way the
audience can quote.

## 11. 동맹·안보 정책 — Alliance and security policy

## Open questions

- **Part A and Part B should probably be two packs, and that is now the first
  decision.** 474 terms is not a deck, it is a curriculum, and the two halves
  have different audiences by register: a 통역병 in a line unit needs Part A and
  can defer Part B; a 통역장교 headed for public affairs or a combined staff
  needs Part B first. Splitting also fixes the section-count problem below —
  ten sections each, which is what the deck page was already being asked to
  hold. Against splitting: 연합/합동 in §10 and 다국적 in §20 are one lesson,
  and §1's naval ranks only matter because of §16. If they do split, they
  should still be one authored source with two registrations, the same way the
  two directions are.
- **The bidirectional registration is the real decision here, and it is a code
  change this PR deliberately does not make.** `VOCAB_PACKS` keys on
  `StudyLanguage`, and `buildPackCardDraft` writes the study side last so it
  wins — which means one authored list of pairs can produce both packs for free:
  registered under `English` the Korean side is the back, registered under
  `Korean` the English side is. No new pack shape is needed, only a source of
  pairs and two derived `VocabPack`s. What needs deciding is whether that shows
  up as two decks with different names, or one deck the app orients from the
  user's study language.
- **Cross-direction saved marking.** `collectSavedTerms` collects the study
  field and `term`, so a user who saved 대대 from the Korean deck will *not* see
  `battalion` marked as saved on the English one. I think that is correct —
  front-to-back in the other direction is a genuinely different card, and this
  pack's whole premise is that both directions are worth drilling — but it is
  the first pack where the two decks overlap by construction, so it should be a
  decision rather than a side effect.
- **Acronyms: which side is the card?** OPCON, FRAGO, MEDEVAC and SOFA are used
  as words, not abbreviations, and Part B made this worse rather than better —
  SLBM, THAAD, ADIZ, ISR, KAMD and KMPR are only ever said as acronyms, and
  nobody has spoken the words "Korea Massive Punishment and Retaliation" aloud
  in a briefing. I have written the English side as `expansion (ACRONYM)`
  throughout, which makes the back long and the review answer ambiguous —
  "fragmentary order" and "FRAGO" are both right. The alternative is the acronym
  alone with the expansion in `context`, which reads better on a card and worse
  in a list. Part B tilts this: for §13 and §14 the acronym clearly *is* the
  answer.
- **Is §7 and §10's barracks vocabulary in scope?** 자대, 말년, 점호 and 소원수리
  are not military English in the doctrinal sense; they are what an interpreter
  is asked about when a US NCO wants to understand a ROK soldier's situation.
  I think that is exactly the job, but it is the least defensible part of the
  list and the easiest to drop as a section.
- **Currency, and who reviews it.** The 2020 renames are in here, and there are
  probably others I have missed. This wants a reviewer who has served recently
  or works a combined staff now — more than any pack we have shipped, this one
  goes stale.
- **`pronounceable` is now clearly yes, and the hints are carrying work they
  should not have to.** Three entries exist mostly as pronunciation warnings —
  군단 corps, 이지스함 Aegis, and by rights 패권 hegemony, which I left out
  because a hint is a poor place to put a stress mark. A Korean interpreter has
  to say "howitzer" out loud and an English-native learner has to say 곡사포;
  the flag exists per pack rather than per direction, so if the mirrored-pack
  approach wins we set it independently on each.
- **One term, several right answers.** Part B breaks the one-to-one assumption
  harder than the two-gloss allowance covers. 북한 is North Korea / the North /
  Pyongyang depending on what you said in the previous sentence, and choosing
  among them across a long passage is the actual skill being tested. I have
  written a single back and pushed the alternatives into the hint, which teaches
  recognition and not production. If there is a card shape that grades "any of
  these", this pack is the argument for it.
- **No usage instances.** The prep glossary this was reconciled against carries
  a real news sentence on roughly three fifths of its entries, with the term
  bolded in place. `context` here is a definition or a warning, which is better
  on a card and worse for collocation — 도발 travels with 감행하다, 태세 with
  격상하다, and nothing in this file teaches that.
- **Twenty sections on the deck page.** Sections are the enrolment unit, so more
  of them means finer control over what a user takes on — but the deck page has
  only ever been laid out against four and six, and twenty is not a list, it is
  a directory. Splitting into two packs halves it; keeping one pack means the
  deck page needs the Part A / Part B level to be real in the UI and not just
  in this file.
